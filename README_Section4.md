## 4. Estimate Existing Active Travel

If the proposed project includes infrastructure elements, the tool estimates the
existing bicycle and pedestrian travel that crosses any part of the project reach
from statistical models of Annual Average Daily Bicycle Traffic (AADBT) and Annual
Average Daily Pedestrian Traffic (AADPT). The models are gradient-boosted decision
trees (LightGBM) trained on bicycle and pedestrian counts from four sources (Table
2) and applied to every link and intersection of the California network. This is
known as a "direct demand" approach to estimating active travel volumes (Miah et
al., 2024a). Bicycle volumes are modeled on network links (road and path segments)
and pedestrian volumes at network nodes (intersections). Explanatory variables
come from the Strava Metro fitness app, network accessibility metrics from the
PeopleForBikes Bicycle Network Analysis (BNA), roadway characteristics from
OpenStreetMap (OSM), built-environment measures from the U.S. EPA Smart Location
Database and National Walkability Index, and PRISM gridded climate data. Two
models are estimated for each mode: one for the existing network, which uses the
Strava activity observed on each link, and one for new facilities, such as a
proposed off-street path, which have no Strava history. The following sections
summarize the data sources and the steps followed to estimate AADBT and AADPT
across the road network of California.

### 4.1 Dependent variables

Count data from four sources were pooled into one bicycle training set and one
pedestrian training set. The sources differ in the kind of locations they cover:
the two statewide annual-estimate datasets are concentrated at busy urban
locations, while the Caltrans and Data Portal counts add low-volume state highway,
trail, and mid-block locations that count programs typically miss.

#### 4.1.1 AADBT from bicycle counts

The bicycle training set combines:

- **Statewide AADBT estimates (Miah et al., 2024b).** Annual average daily bicycle
  traffic produced by the UC Berkeley SafeTREC large-scale AADBT methodology. These
  form the largest share of the bicycle data and are concentrated at on-street
  urban locations.
- **Caltrans Active Transportation Count Dataset.** Continuous counts from the
  Caltrans district pilot program on the state highway system. These sites are
  predominantly low volume but continuously observed.
- **California Active Transportation Data Portal.** Count sites contributed by
  agencies statewide, most of them short-duration manual counts (typically
  two-hour peak-period counts), plus some permanent automated counters. The Data
  Portal adds trail and mid-block locations absent from the other sources.

#### 4.1.2 AADPT from pedestrian counts

The pedestrian training set combines:

- **California State Highway System pedestrian exposure model (Griswold et al.,
  2019).** Annual intersection crossing volume estimates, normalized to 2016
  population, divided by 365 to obtain AADPT. A limitation of this source is that
  it is itself an estimate (short-term counts factored to annual volumes) and is
  thus subject to unknown errors. These records carry no count year and were
  assigned to 2018 for pooling with the other sources.
- **Caltrans Active Transportation Count Dataset.** Continuous pedestrian counts
  on the state highway system, with directional legs combined into a total
  crossing volume at each location.
- **California Active Transportation Data Portal.** Agency-contributed pedestrian
  counts, mostly short-duration manual counts.

Figure 2 shows the pedestrian count locations by source. A single statewide model
is estimated for each mode and model type; the models are not split by urban and
rural area, because the explanatory variables themselves capture the differences
between those settings.

#### 4.2.1 Strava Metro

Strava Metro provided annual (2023) bicycle trip counts for every edge of its
California network, delivered by Caltrans district. The Strava edge network is
built on OSM and forms the network the tool models: approximately eight million
links. The total annual trip count on a link is a predictor in the
existing-network models for both modes. At a node, the Strava predictor is half the summed Strava volume of the links that meet there (the trips passing through the node).

A new facility has no Strava history, so the models also use the Strava activity
surrounding each location ("ambient" Strava). Link volumes were rasterized to a
100 m grid, and moving-window sums give the total Strava trips within four
non-overlapping rings around each location: 0–250 m, 250–500 m, 500–1,000 m, and
1,000–2,000 m. Non-overlapping rings reduce the correlation between the four
measures. The gridded sums were checked against exact buffer sums for a sample of
locations (correlation of 0.99 or higher at every ring). Ring sums are
log-transformed. Ambient Strava is available for any location, including a
proposed path, and carries much of the demand signal in the new-facility models.

#### 4.2.2 Network accessibility metrics

The network accessibility metrics are census-block-level outputs of PeopleForBikes'
Bicycle Network Analysis (BNA) tool. For each census block, the BNA counts the
population, jobs, and destinations (schools, colleges, doctors, pharmacies,
retail, supermarkets, parks, trails, community centers, and transit stations) in
other blocks that can be reached by bicycle, both over the full street network
and over low-stress connections only. The low- and high-stress classification is
loosely based on classifications designed to differentiate road environments that
are comfortable and accommodating to bicycling (low stress) from those that are
not (high stress) (Furth and Mekuria, 2013; Mekuria et al., 2012). Each link takes
the values of the census block containing its midpoint, along with the block's
housing units. Each node takes the average of its connecting links.

#### 4.2.3 Roadway characteristics

Roadway characteristics are included in both the bicycle and pedestrian models and
were derived directly from OSM tags. Two separate classifications describe each
link:

- **Bicycle facility type** (from the OSM `highway`, `cycleway`, and `bicycle`
  tags), ranked from most to least protective: separated path, buffered lane, bike
  lane, marked shared lane, quiet (residential) street, shared arterial, and other.
- **Road functional class** (from the OSM `highway` tag): Major Road (motorway,
  trunk, primary), Minor Road (secondary, tertiary), and Local Road (residential
  streets, paths, and all other ways).

Facility type describes the cycling infrastructure; functional class describes the
road hierarchy. A bike lane can be on a major or a minor road, so the two carry
different information. Surface type is coded as paved or unpaved. Speed limit is
the posted `maxspeed` where tagged; otherwise a default for the road class is used
(65 mph motorway; 45 mph trunk and primary; 35 mph secondary; 25 mph tertiary and
residential; 15 mph separated paths). At a node, the roadway variables describe
the most significant of the connecting links: the highest speed limit, the highest
functional class, the most protective facility type, and paved if any connecting
link is paved.

#### 4.2.4 Built environment

Two built-environment measures come from the U.S. EPA Smart Location Database:
gross population density (D1B) and street intersection density (D3B). The EPA
National Walkability Index, a composite of intersection density, proximity to
transit, and land-use mix, is included as a summary measure of walkability. Each
measure is taken at the census block group containing the link midpoint, and each
node takes the average of its connecting links.

#### 4.2.5 Climate

Both the bicycle and pedestrian models include annual precipitation and the annual
means of daily minimum and maximum temperature. These came from the PRISM 4 km
gridded climate surfaces for 2023, sampled at each link midpoint. A gridded
surface replaced the previous nearest-weather-station approach, which assigned
distant and unrepresentative stations to many locations (particularly in the
foothills) once the models were applied to the full statewide network.

### 4.3 Data processing and modeling procedure

All sources of count data were pooled into two sets, one for training the
bicycle models and one for training the pedestrian models. Records lacking
location information or volumes were removed. Data Portal sites within 30 meters
of a site in the statewide AADBT or pedestrian datasets were dropped as duplicates.
Data Portal records that duplicate the Caltrans permanent counters were also
dropped, keeping the Caltrans copy. Most of the data come from high-volume sites.
The Caltrans Active Transportation Count Dataset is unique in its sourcing of
low-volume locations on the state highway system, and over-prediction at low
volume sites is a commonly recognized source of error in direct demand models
(Miah et al., 2024a).

Because count durations range from a few hours to years, we standardized them to
a common annual average. We expanded short-term counts in two steps. (1) A count
spanning only part of a day was scaled to a full day using an hour-of-day activity
profile (by mode, for weekdays and weekends) from the Data Portal's permanent
counters, weighted by the specific hours the short count observed. (2) Daily
volumes were then adjusted for seasonality using monthly factors from sites in the
same region with at least nine months of data. For count sources that are
themselves annual average estimates (Griswold et al., 2019; Miah et al., 2024b),
we assumed the estimates to be correct and made no adjustments.

We matched each count site to the network. Pedestrian counts were matched to the
nearest node. Bicycle counts reported without direction were matched to the
nearest link. Directional bicycle counts, as at an intersection counted by
approach, were summed by street axis (north–south, east–west, and the diagonals)
and each axis total was assigned to the nearest link within 50 meters whose
bearing matches that axis. This gives each street at an intersection its own
bi-directional volume.

We modeled the bicycle and pedestrian average annual daily volumes using
gradient-boosted decision trees (LightGBM) with a Tweedie (compound
Poisson–gamma) objective function. We selected the Tweedie objective because the
volumes are non-negative, include many values near zero, and are heavily
right-skewed. For each mode, we estimated two models. The first (Track A, the
existing-network model) includes the link-specific Strava Metro volume as a
predictor and is used to predict volume on all existing network features. The
second (Track B, the new-facility model) excludes the link-specific Strava Metro
volume and relies on the ambient Strava activity surrounding the site and the
other contextual variables. It is used to predict volume on user-drawn paths and
nodes, which represent entirely new network features without Strava history. The
bicycle models were estimated at the bi-directional link volume level and the
pedestrian models at the total crossing volume (node) level. Model performance is
evaluated by volume class, using terciles of low, medium, and high volume (Table
3a).

[Table 3a: Volume class (tercile) definitions]

We selected hyperparameters by a grid search under spatial 5-fold
cross-validation with early stopping, with folds grouped by count location so that
observations of the same location never appear in both training and test sets. We
first tuned the Tweedie variance power, the number of tree leaves, the minimum
observations per leaf, and feature subsampling. With those four parameters held at
their selected values, we then tuned regularization (L1 and L2 penalty terms, row
subsampling, and maximum tree depth) to guard against overfitting. Selection
followed a priority order across three metrics: first, accuracy in predicting the
volume class (tercile), because of the wide variation in activity across count
locations and the need to get the approximate volume right more than the precise
volume at a site; second, a low rate of severe misclassification (a low-volume
site predicted as high, or the reverse); and third, the lowest root-mean-square
error (RMSE). Configurations whose class accuracy differed by less than one
standard error (about one percentage point) were treated as statistically
indistinguishable and separated by the secondary metrics. This was done for each
mode and model type, resulting in four sets of hyperparameters (Table 3b).

The bicycle models use a heavier-tailed Tweedie power (1.9), which was best on all
three metrics. The pedestrian models use a lower power (1.7): the higher powers
improved class accuracy by well under one standard error while worsening both
severe misclassification and RMSE. Regularization produced small gains and was
kept only where it improved the secondary metrics without reducing accuracy. The
bicycle existing-network model uses no regularization. Performance was insensitive
to the hyperparameters across a broad range, indicating that the models are not
overfitting. Final models were trained on all counts with 600 trees at a learning
rate of 0.05.

[Table 3b: Selected model hyperparameters]

We validated the final model specifications by spatial 10-fold cross-validation,
grouped by count location, and evaluated performance first on predicting the
volume class, then on severe misclassification and absolute error.

### 4.6 Application of models for estimating active travel networkwide

The existing-network models (Track A) were applied across the entire California
network in the tool, approximately eight million links, to predict AADBT on every
link and AADPT (crossing volume) at every node. The predictions are then shared
between the two layers: each node is assigned half the summed bicycle volume of its connecting links (the bicyclists passing through it), and each link is assigned the average pedestrian volume of its two end nodes. When a user selects a link or node on the map, the tool reads the corresponding precomputed AADBT or AADPT.

For a proposed new facility drawn by the user, the tool uses the new-facility
models (Track B). Their predictions are precomputed for every census block, with
the facility characteristics set to those of a new off-street path (a paved,
separated path on a local road with a 15 mph speed). The facility variables carry
only a few percent of model gain, so each block's prediction is effectively a
function of its location. The tool spatially joins a drawn path and its nodes to
the underlying census blocks and reads the predicted bicycle and pedestrian
volumes.

Because the models draw on a wide variety of variables relating to accessibility,
activity, roadway, and built environment, the estimates provide context-sensitive
predictions of current active travel.
